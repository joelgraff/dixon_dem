# Dixon Vernacular — General Look Reference

This document covers the "everyday fabric" of Dixon: what fills the space between the named landmarks. Sources: Dixon Downtown Historic District NRHP summary (Wikipedia), a first-hand 2002 downtown photo walking tour (southpolestation.com), an aerial panorama, and ~15 street-level Wikimedia Commons photos of First St, Second St, Third St, Hennepin Ave, Monroe Ave, and assorted downtown storefronts (all cited below).

## Overall town geography
Dixon sits at a bend of the Rock River; downtown occupies the flat riverside blocks right at the bend (courthouse, Galena Ave, Nachusa House, theatre, bridge, dam all within ~3 blocks of each other and the river). Residential neighborhoods extend south and east from downtown in a fairly regular grid, with mature tree canopy so dense from above that streets nearly disappear under it in aerial photos. The land beyond town is flat-to-gently-rolling farmland visible at the horizon in wide/aerial shots.

## Downtown commercial core (Galena Ave, First St, Second St, Hennepin Ave)
- **Building type:** attached, party-wall commercial blocks, mostly **2 storeys** (occasionally 3–5, e.g. Nachusa House), built mostly **1850s–1920s**. Italianate predominates for the oldest (1850s–1880s) buildings; **Commercial style** and revival styles (Neoclassical, Beaux-Arts) common after 1900.
- **Brick color:** warm red-brown to buff-tan face brick, `#9A5B45` to `#C4A876` range — mix rather than uniform; corner/civic buildings tend toward lighter buff/cream, ordinary storefronts toward red-brown.
- **Cornices:** **corbelled brick cornices** on the older Italianate blocks (stepped brick coursing, sometimes a pressed-metal cornice cap); flatter parapets with cast-stone or terra-cotta banding on early-1900s buildings (see the Streetlight/Building B&W reference — note the decorative terra-cotta tile band and dentil cornice on a downtown corner building).
- **Storefronts:** large plate-glass ground-floor display windows, some retaining cast-iron or stone storefront piers from the 19th century; many have been **modernized** in the mid-20th-century with brick-veneer pier cladding, back-lit channel-letter or applied-metal-letter signage (e.g. "Venier's Jewelers" in gold script on brown brick with a black-and-gold canvas awning), and flat canopy awnings. Expect a mix: a handful of ornate 19th-century cast-iron storefronts next to 1960s-70s "brick box" remodels — **do not make every storefront uniformly ornate.**
- **Awnings:** striped canvas (green-and-cream stripe seen on one storefront) alongside plain solid-color flat metal/canvas canopies.
- **Upper floors:** regular window rhythm, plain stone or brick lintels/sills, occasionally a decorative panel or cartouche at the parapet.
- **Signage era mix:** painted ghost-signs on brick side walls are plausible (common in small Midwest downtowns of this vintage) even if not directly documented here.

## Residential streets
- **House styles, mixed within the same block:** **Queen Anne** (steep cross-gables, wraparound porches, turned porch posts, decorative shingle bands — late 1800s) and **Craftsman/bungalow** (lower-slung, broad porches, early 1900s) predominate per the historic district summary; reference photos also show a **mid-century Prairie-influenced ranch** (flat overhanging eaves, buff limestone cladding) mixed into older blocks, and a small Craftsman-Prairie foursquare here and there — Dixon's residential fabric spans roughly **1870s–1960s** within a few blocks.
- **Siding colors:** predominantly **white** or **pale pastel** clapboard/lap siding — observed examples: white, powder blue, pale yellow, light gray, cream — with **contrasting gray or white trim**. Avoid saturated/bright colors; keep it soft and slightly chalky/weathered.
- **Porches:** near-universal on pre-1930s houses — full-width or wraparound, turned or square Tuscan columns, painted white, often with a working screen door.
- **Roofs:** steep cross-gable or hip roofs on Victorians (gray-brown composition shingle), lower-pitched on bungalows/ranches.
- **Chimneys:** tall, slender, red brick, usually mid-roof.
- **Lots & setback:** narrow-to-medium in-town lots, modest front-yard setbacks (~6–8 m), mature shade trees frequently overhanging the street from both sides, forming a canopy.
- **Streetscape:** concrete sidewalks set back from the curb behind a **grass parkway/verge strip** (not curb-attached sidewalks); asphalt streets, visibly aged with **tar-sealed cracks and patch repairs** (very consistent across reference photos — this is a strong, cheap authenticity detail); wood utility poles (not decorative) with overhead wire runs are the residential-street norm, contrasted with ornate cast-iron poles downtown.

## Street furniture
- **Downtown lamp posts:** ornate black cast-iron poles with **double-globe luminaires**, hanging flower baskets in summer (seen flanking the Dixon Arch and along Galena Ave).
- **Residential streets:** plain wood utility poles, no decorative streetlights — just occasional cobra-head lights on the wood poles.
- **Traffic signals:** modern **mast-arm mounted** box signal heads (not span-wire) at major downtown intersections (confirmed at Galena Ave & the arch, and Galena & 2nd by the theatre).
- **Street signs:** standard green-on-white Illinois municipal signage (e.g. "2ND STREET").
- **Curbs/gutters:** downtown has a **brick paver strip** along the gutter line at some intersections (a nice period texture band against plain gray concrete sidewalks); residential streets are plain concrete curb and sidewalk.
- **Stop signs:** standard red octagon on a plain post at unsignalized residential intersections.
- Parking meters: not confirmed in any reference photo — do not assume they exist; free curb parking appears to be the norm today. Planters, benches, and trash cans: not clearly documented; keep minimal/generic if included.

## Vegetation
Dense, mature deciduous canopy throughout residential areas (oak/maple-type broad shade trees dominate street rows), ornamental evergreens (blue spruce, pine) common as specimen trees in front yards, foundation shrubs/hedges at porches, mown lawns everywhere. Riverbank/park areas have looser mixed brush and shade trees. For Illinois specifically, expect: **sugar maple, red maple, white/bur oak, honey locust (a common modern street-tree replacement for the dying ashes), American elm remnants, and blue spruce/white pine as ornamentals.**

## Riverfront
Heritage Crossing / Riverfront Park area near the Galena Ave bridge: paved multi-use trail, mown grass banks, some retaining walls near the dam/bridge, simple pipe-rail or cable fencing at drop-offs, marina/dock structures near "Oppold Marina" upriver. Orange safety buoys mark the dam hazard zone (see `dixon_dam.md`).

## Typical vehicles
Reference photos (2007–2022) show ordinary contemporary American sedans, minivans, and pickups — a Ford Mustang, Chevrolet/Honda sedans, a Dodge Caravan-type minivan, a Chevrolet pickup, a marked Dixon police cruiser. No unusual vehicle culture — treat Dixon as visually generic small-Midwest-town automotive: mid-size sedans, crossovers/SUVs, and full-size pickups dominate; keep vehicle paint palette muted (white, silver, black, dark red/maroon, navy) rather than bright.
