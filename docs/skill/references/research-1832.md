# Dixon's Ferry, 1832: research for chapter 2

## Verified from the texts (read 2026-11-08)

Texts: Stevens, *The Black Hawk War* (1903), ch. XIX and pp. on Dixon's
account books; *History of Lee County* (H.H. Hill, 1881), Black Hawk War
chapter (genealogytrails transcription); AHGP "Accounts from the books of
John Dixon" and "Children of John and Rebecca Dixon" (sources.yaml S2, and a
companion page to S1).

**Black Hawk's band at the ferry: three accounts that disagree.**
- *Lee County 1881:* Dixon was warned and "would have abandoned his home, and
  sought safety elsewhere", but the Winnebago chief **Pachinka (Crane)** told
  him the Winnebago held the land the Sauk had to cross and "would not dare
  injure the white friends of his nation". So Dixon was there when the band
  passed "early in May". He "so arranged his family and hired help as to
  ascertain the force of the hostiles ... without exciting their suspicions":
  600 men. The band camped at a spring a few hundred yards above the ferry.
  Black Hawk remembered Mrs. Dixon's kindness the winter before, when he, a
  Rock Island chief and the Prophet held council with the Potawatomi at the
  ferry and she served the chiefs at her table, "and even sat down and ate and
  drank with them".
- *Stevens:* the band "camped one night near the Dixon cabin", and Black Hawk,
  Neapope and the Prophet "ate with the family, Mrs. Dixon waiting upon them
  in a manner so courteous as completely to captivate Black Hawk". The family
  counted 800 warriors and reported it to the troops, who arrived May 12.
- *AHGP, Children page:* **Dixon was away at Galena.** Rebecca was home alone
  with the children when the band "crowded in, filling the house". She sent
  for **Old Crane**, who came "and with the aid of **Wischick**, one of the
  chiefs of the Sacs and Foxes, got the intruders out". At Crane's suggestion
  she cooked for the chiefs, and **Black Hawk, Wischick and Kaapapi** dined,
  while the rest camped "at the large spring on the south bank".

**The family in 1832 (corrects the first pass).** "Afterwards it was thought
desirable for Mrs. Dixon and her children to go to **Galena** ... and she did
so." **"During this period two of the small children of Mr. and Mrs. Dixon,
whose names cannot now be learned, died at Galena."** (AHGP Children.) Stevens
quotes a captain in June: Dixon "had sent his family to Galena for safety".
The house was never closed: Rebecca was there part of the time, and the sons
James and Elijah kept the doors open. (The flight to **Peoria County**, with
the house "broken up", was **spring 1833**, when the Winnebago grew restive
and Crane "could not talk so assuring of his own tribe as in 1832". Lee County
1881.)

**John Dixon in the war.** Stayed at the ferry, then went with the army "as
commissary, scout and otherwise", "in the personal service of
Lieutenant-Colonel Taylor and messed with him and his officers"; held the
beef contract to Bad Axe ("Major of the Steer Battalion"). Met two Indians
off the trail, lowered his gun and greeted them in Winnebago; they had
dispatches for Atkinson and were afraid to approach the soldiers. In June he
hauled a scouting company's corn in his wagon toward Galena (Stevens).
Officers stayed at his house for weeks, hunting and fishing; "the little
account book was at all times open"; Lieutenants Davis and Anderson "were
always cash customers". After the war he distributed the Winnebago's rations
under Scott's treaty.

**The place.** Stevens: a ferry ("the simple flat-bottomed affair") and a
90-foot log cabin in three sections. AHGP: two parts, Ogee's one-story cabin
and Dixon's two-story addition, with a 10-12 ft hall between; west room the
family's (two beds, chairs, a long table); east room for travelers and hired
help, **four beds, one in each corner**; the store in the east, two-story
part (powder, lead, shot, tobacco, pipes, cloth, blankets, guns, beads,
traps). The ferry landing was at the foot of today's Peoria Avenue. The post
office was in Dixon's cabin until James's cabin (1834) got a 10x10 lean-to.
Dixon was postmaster of **Ogee's Ferry** from Sept 29, 1830; the name became
**Dixon's Ferry in 1834**. Fort Dixon: two blockhouses inside a sod and earth
breastwork 4.5 ft high, on the north bank a few rods west of the ferry;
Captain Palmer's infantry company guarded the ferry (Lee County 1881).

**The account books.** Two books survive. Ferriage for a man and horse 25¢,
a meal 25¢, a night's lodging 25¢. Many bills never paid. **Ogee's account**
runs over $500, with "2 caps for sons", "2 pr. mittens for sons" and **"cash
to Margaret to go to Fort $2"**. Indian customers named in the book include
**"Patchunka Chief Crane"** (blue and red cloth, shirts, beads, tobacco,
powder, combs, spears, paint), Crane's son, Crane's brother, Chief Jarro (who
paid), Old Blue Socks's son, Squirrel Cheeks, Good Singer, Blinky, and "W.
Lock old man (Ogee says he is good)". May 10 and 15, 1832: Col. Zachary Taylor,
$11.50, "settled by note". May 20, 1832: to General Atkinson's quartermaster,
blankets, guns and a rifle, $360.50. Col. William S. Hamilton, Alexander
Hamilton's son, ran up $339.55 from July 1832 and never paid.

**Other scene-sized details (Lee County 1881).** The first man back from
Stillman's Run was "a Kentucky lawyer" who said everyone else was dead;
stragglers came in all night with the same story. "Justice compels the
impartial historian to record that the whites were the aggressors": Stillman's
men, many drunk, fired on Black Hawk's flag of truce. Capt. John Dement had
come back from a scout advising that the band could be talked into going home.
On May 19 dispatch riders guided by **John Winters, the mail contractor**,
were ambushed at Buffalo Grove. Part of the Hall sisters' ransom was paid to
the Winnebago chief **Whirling Thunder at Dixon's Ferry**. Lincoln to Robert
Anderson in 1861: "You mustered me into the U.S. service as a high private
... at Dixon's Ferry."

**Ogee.** Stevens: Dixon brought Ogee, "a half-breed", from Peoria to the
river in 1828, and Ogee ran the ferry unlicensed until a Jo Daviess County
license of Dec 7, 1829; by 1830 "the restraint of a ferryman's life had become
so exceedingly irksome to one of his nomadic nature" that Dixon took it over.
Stevens's note on the name: **"Pronounced Ozha."** Atkinson's order of May 11,
1832 is dated "Near **Marie de Ogee**", a place on the lower Rock River.

## First pass (search summaries; superseded where the above differs)

### Timeline at the ferry

- **Early April 1832:** Black Hawk's band (the "British Band" of Sauk and
  allies) recrosses the Mississippi (April 5 or 6, conflict) and goes up the
  Rock River.
- **Late April or early May (conflict):** the band passes Dixon's Ferry and
  camps at a spring a few hundred yards above it. Dixon put them at about 600
  men and later said they came to his house (Local, genealogytrails Lee
  County). Dixon was warned of their approach and **chose to stay**. A
  Winnebago (Ho-Chunk) chief, Pachinka, is said to have assured him the
  Winnebago held the land the band would cross and his friends would not be
  harmed (Local, single blog source).
- **About May 12:** militia gather at Dixon's Ferry. Lincoln's company camps
  about a mile west (repo note, docs/references/lincoln_statue.md).
- **May 13-14, Stillman's Run:** about 275 militia under Majors Stillman and
  Bailey ride out from Dixon's Ferry to Old Man's Creek. They are routed at
  dusk on the 14th, and the rest flee about 30 miles back to Dixon's,
  "spreading news of a terrible slaughter". Around a dozen killed (11 or 12,
  conflict). Captain John Giles Adams makes a stand on a hill and dies there.
- **May 15:** Whiteside's force, Lincoln's company with it, reaches the battle
  site and buries the dead (date and Lincoln's presence under question).
- **Mid to late May:** settlers across northern Illinois and the lead region
  flee or fort up. Rebecca and the children go to Galena (see above; the
  Peoria County flight was 1833).
- **May 16:** Shabbona (Potawatomi) rides across northern Illinois warning
  settlers. At Indian Creek his warning is ignored. **May 20 or 21 (conflict),
  Indian Creek:** fifteen settlers killed, and Sylvia and Rachel Hall taken
  (ransomed through Henry Gratiot, freed June 1 at Blue Mound).
- **May 22:** General Atkinson makes Dixon's Ferry his command post (central
  between Forts Dearborn, Armstrong and Clark). **Fort Dixon** is built by
  Zachary Taylor's regulars on the north bank to store rations and munitions.
  How long it stood is a conflict (abandoned 1832, or stood to about 1843).
- **May 22-24, the St. Vrain party:** a party leaves Dixon's for Galena, finds
  William Durley's body at Buffalo Grove, and comes back to Dixon's overnight.
  Atkinson returns with dispatches for Fort Armstrong and sends Indian agent
  Felix St. Vrain with them. On May 24 St. Vrain and three others are killed
  near Kellogg's Grove; three survivors reach Galena on the 26th.
- **May 27:** Lincoln's first enlistment ends (mustered out near Ottawa;
  Robert Anderson is said to have mustered him in and out, Local, 1890 Dixon
  Telegraph). **June 16:** a later muster-out.
- **June:** express riders run between Galena and the army at Dixon's Ferry
  (four couriers from Galena reach Apple River Fort). June 25-26, second
  battle at Kellogg's Grove; Early's company helps bury the dead.
- **August 2, Bad Axe:** the war ends with the slaughter of Black Hawk's band
  at the Mississippi. Most of the Rock River Winnebago country is ceded
  afterward.

### John Dixon in 1832

- Postmaster; owns the ferry outright from January 1832 (C8).
- Stays when warned, with Winnebago friends' assurance (Local).
- Sends his family to Galena, where two small children die (see above).
- Assists the army's commissary and **goes with the army to the end of the
  campaign** (Stevens, via search). His books record goods furnished to the
  army in May 1832 (S2, ahgp).
- Called "Nachusa" by the Winnebago: "white hair" or "pale hair" (conflict
  on the exact form). Later given a 160-acre bounty land warrant for his
  service.

### The Ogees

- Madeline Ogee received two sections at Paw Paw Grove under the 1829 Treaty
  of Prairie du Chien. The section was later sold to David Town for **$1,000 in
  silver** (marker; date not given). Waubonsee's Potawatomi lived in the grove
  1824-1836 and were removed in 1836.
- Nothing found yet on where Joseph Ogee was in 1832.

## Things to keep in mind

- **Repair restores the record.** In a war year, putting history right can
  mean letting real deaths happen. Decided 2026-11-08: that cost is allowed
  (the user's touchstone is *The City on the Edge of Forever*: Edith Keeler
  has to die), but it isn't required, and chapter 2's break is built on
  something constructive.
- **The Sauk, Ho-Chunk and Potawatomi are people with their own reasons**,
  not scenery or a threat. Black Hawk's band was trying to go home to
  Saukenuk; the war ended in a massacre of that band. Dixon's own story is
  bound up with Winnebago friendship. Madeline's people include Potawatomi who
  sided with the United States and still lost their land four years later.
- Planted in chapter 1: Eli ("the war's coming back"), Dixon on Saukenuk and
  "Eighteen thirty-two? That's two years off", Madeline's "Nobody is going to
  burn this ferry", Bogardus's burned boat, Margaret's "the militia came
  through here in 1832, Lincoln among them".
