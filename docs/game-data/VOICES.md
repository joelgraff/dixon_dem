# Voices

How each character talks. The narrator writes in complete, flowing sentences
(see "Prose" in docs/skill/references/interaction-design.md); characters
don't have to. Speech should sound like the person: their origin, their work,
their temper and what they want from you. Two characters should never be
interchangeable on the page.

Rules for everyone:

- Voice comes from rhythm, word choice and what a person leaves out. Never
  from respelled dialect ("goin'", "ye", "dat").
- 1830 Illinois is American frontier country. Avoid the stock British and
  literary turns that make everyone sound alike: "I'll not", "I've a",
  "Mind you", "you'll be the one", "I'd sooner", "ought", "a good deal".
  A character may use one of them only if the sheet below gives it to them.
- Fragments, interruptions, restarts and unanswered questions are allowed in
  speech, where they fit the speaker.
- A speaker's lines should be recognisable without the speech tag.

---

## Joseph Ogee (the ferryman)

**Who:** French and Native ancestry, a ferryman since 1828, married into
Madeline's Potawatomi family. Deep in debt, proud of his river, warm under it.

**How he talks:** Short, practical sentences. English is a working language
for him, so word order sometimes leans French ("That one, I don't trust").
Dry humor, often at his own expense. Calls the ferry "she". Rarely asks
questions; tells you what is.

**Uses:** plain verbs, river words (pole, flat, current, line), the odd French
word under his breath (bon, mon Dieu), "eh" as a tag.

**Never:** long explanations, "Mind you", "I'll not", formal courtesy.

**Sounds like:** "I called for somebody who could handle a canoe, and the river
sent me somebody who needed one."

## John Dixon (the mail contractor)

**Who:** Born in New York, carries the mail from Peoria to Galena. A careful
man of business with a family in a wagon and no silver.

**How he talks:** The one character who speaks in measured, complete
sentences. Polite and a little formal; uses titles ("Mr. Ogee"). Weighs things
aloud. Understates trouble.

**Uses:** business words (terms, lease, rent, account, obliged), "I'd be
obliged", "as it happens".

**Never:** slang, oaths, raising his voice.

**Sounds like:** "I've lent money to half the men on this road, and it's a
funny thing how none of it is in my pocket today."

## Asa (the teamster)

**Who:** Hauls flour and tools to the Galena lead diggings, four or five runs
a season. Upland Southern stock, Kentucky by way of southern Illinois. Blunt,
impatient, fair in a deal, soft about his sister's boy (but he won't say so).

**How he talks:** Short and blunt. Drops the subject when it's obvious ("Got
flour for Galena." "Bar broke an hour back."). Loose grammar: "ain't", "you
seen", "them wheels", "never come home". Counts and bargains in plain numbers.
Calls his wagon "she". Swears offstage (the narrator says so; he doesn't on
the page).

**Uses:** reckon, fixing to, fella, yonder, a sight, plumb, square (for fair),
"Silver I got."

**Never:** "I'll not", "I've a", "ought", "Mind", anything that sounds read
from a book. No long sentences unless he's angry, and then they run together
with "and".

**Sounds like:** "Silver I got. What I ain't got is a ferry."

## Eli (Asa's nephew)

**Who:** Sixteen or so, carrying his dead father's flintlock to the diggings.
Nervous, eager, trying to sound older than he is.

**How he talks:** Run-on sentences joined with "and", stops and restarts
mid-thought, quotes his uncle and his pa ("Uncle says...", "Pa always
said..."). Polite in a hurry ("Oh. Sure.").

**Uses:** "anyhow", "I guess", "honest", "back yonder".

**Never:** a calm, finished paragraph.

**Sounds like:** "Uncle says a man can make more in a summer at the diggings
than in five years farming. Uncle says a lot of things."

## Rolette's agent

**Who:** A trader's man from Galena, sent to collect on Ogee's notes or take
the boat. Not cruel, not interested in anyone's trouble.

**How he talks:** Clipped and exact. Numbers, dates, terms. No contractions.
Calls everyone "Mr." or "friend". Polite in a way that closes the
conversation. Short sentences; sometimes just the facts in a row.

**Uses:** goods, note, due, terms, silver, today.

**Never:** a personal opinion, sympathy, slang, a raised voice.

**Sounds like:** "Mr. Dixon has paper, and paper is not silver."

## Madeline Ogee

**Who:** Potawatomi, daughter of the trader LaSallier, Ogee's wife. Keeps the
cabin, the boys and the peace with her people. Wary of strangers.

**How she talks:** As few words as will do. Statements, not explanations.
Answers a question with a question when she doesn't trust it. Warmth shows in
what she does, not what she says.

**Never:** small talk, flattery, more than two sentences unless something
matters to her (her father, the ferry).

**Sounds like:** "Nobody is going to burn this ferry."

## Rebecca Dixon

**Who:** John's wife, living out of a wagon with the children since April,
waiting for a roof.

**How she talks:** Short, tired, dry. Practical complaints with a joke folded
in. Calls her husband "John". Raises her voice at the boys.

**Sounds like:** "I'm tired. Ask me again when there's a floor."

## James Dixon

**Who:** Nineteen, drives the mail for his father, has lost the mail key and
is frightened his father will find out.

**How he talks:** Earnest and a little stiff ("Father"). Says more than he
means to when he's worried, then stops himself.

**Sounds like:** "Father need never know."

## The boys (Ogee's and Dixon's)

Children. Short, quarrelsome, all at once. They interrupt each other.

## Margaret (the present day)

**Who:** A retired local volunteer, thirty-one years of Saturdays at the
Historic Center. Kind, curious, a little lonely.

**How she talks:** Contemporary Midwestern, chatty, self-deprecating. Hedges
("I suppose", "if you ask me", "Silly, I know"). Trails off when something
unsettles her. Says "not to think about it" (the refrain; see "Prose").

**Never:** frontier or old-fashioned turns of phrase.

**Sounds like:** "Thirty-one years of Saturdays. Someone has to know where the
stapler is."
